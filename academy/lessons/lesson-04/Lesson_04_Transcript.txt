SCENE 1: Values, Identity, and Personal Truth
Welcome to Lesson Four: Values, Identity, and Personal Truth. Clients often arrive saying, I do not feel like myself, or I have built a life that looks good but does not feel like mine. A coach should not hand them a new identity. The work is to help them hear what matters beneath roles, expectations, and performance, then turn that clarity into honest, responsible action.

SCENE 2: Your learning journey
We will begin by making values practical rather than decorative. Then we will distinguish identity from roles, labels, achievements, and other people's expectations. You will learn to recognize alignment gaps, coach a client through competing values, and help personal truth become a measured action instead of an impulsive declaration. Six decisions, a complete client case, workbook practice, and a graded assessment will help you apply the material.

SCENE 3: Values are qualities of living
A value is a quality a person wants to express through choices. Honesty, care, courage, stability, creativity, faith, justice, belonging, and freedom are examples. A goal can be completed. A value keeps guiding how the goal is pursued. A client may reach a promotion and still want to lead with fairness. She may end a relationship and still want to communicate with dignity. Values are directions for living, not trophies to collect.

SCENE 4: Why generic value lists fall short
A client can circle twenty beautiful words and learn very little. Some words describe what she admires in other people. Others reflect family, religious, cultural, or professional expectations. The most useful values become clearer when a real tradeoff appears. When time, approval, money, or comfort is limited, what does the client protect? Coaching moves from a word list to lived evidence, without treating one imperfect choice as proof of character.

SCENE 6: The Values Evidence Test
Use the Values Evidence Test to move from aspiration to practice. Ask what the value looks like in observable behavior. Identify habits and relationships that support it. Explore the healthy cost the client is willing to carry, such as discomfort, time, or disappointing someone. Then identify one current choice that could express the value. This prevents values from becoming vague promises and helps clients recognize that integrity sometimes includes a real tradeoff.

SCENE 7: Values can compete without one being wrong
Many difficult decisions are not value against bad behavior. They are value against value. Freedom may compete with security. Honesty may feel in tension with kindness. Achievement may collide with rest. Belonging may conflict with individuality. The coach should not declare the superior value. Help the client name both, assess the context, and decide which expression creates the greatest integrity now while honoring the other as responsibly as possible.

SCENE 8: A values hierarchy is contextual
Clients sometimes believe their values must stay in one permanent ranking. In reality, context matters. Health may need to lead during recovery. Stability may lead during a financial emergency. Creativity may move forward after years of neglect. This does not mean values are random. It means the client makes a conscious priority for a particular season and decision. Ask what must lead now, what must not be abandoned, and when the priority should be reviewed.

SCENE 9: Roles are real—but they are not the whole self
Roles organize responsibility and belonging. They can be deeply meaningful. Trouble begins when a role becomes the only permitted answer to who am I. A parent may forget desire outside caregiving. A leader may confuse worth with performance. A partner may shrink preferences to preserve harmony. Coaching helps the client honor her roles while recovering the values, capacities, interests, and voice that exist within and beyond them.

SCENE 11: The Identity Constellation
Invite the client to build an Identity Constellation. Map roles and relationships, values and commitments, strengths and capacities, interests and curiosities, formative experiences, communities, and hopes. No single point defines the whole person. The map becomes especially useful during divorce, retirement, empty nesting, career transition, caregiving, or relocation, when one visible role changes and the client fears that the self has disappeared with it.

SCENE 12: Identity statements can open—or close—the future
Identity language can sound permanent. I am bad at conflict. I am the responsible one. I am not creative. A coach can respectfully move from verdict to practice: what happens in conflict now, what are you learning, and what would one different behavior look like? This is not positive thinking. The client keeps an honest account of current patterns while making room for development. Identity becomes a living practice rather than a locked sentence.

SCENE 13: Inherited expectations shape identity
Clients develop inside families, cultures, faith communities, schools, workplaces, and gender expectations. These influences may offer wisdom, belonging, pressure, or all three. The coach should not mock tradition or assume independence is always the healthiest answer. Ask what message the client received, what it protected or made possible, what cost it now creates, and which parts she consciously chooses to carry forward.

SCENE 14: The Should Audit
The Roux Life Should Audit turns vague pressure into something the client can examine. Write the sentence beginning with I should. Identify where it came from. Explore how following it has helped, protected, or connected the client. Name the present cost. Then decide whether to choose it freely, revise it for the current season, or release it. The coach does not command rebellion. The client makes an informed decision about what remains hers.

SCENE 16: Alignment is the distance between values and behavior
An alignment gap appears when repeated behavior does not express a stated value. A client values connection but gives every evening to work. She values honesty but agrees while resentment grows. The coach names the gap without turning it into hypocrisy or shame. What does the value mean? What pattern is occurring? What consequence follows? What repairing action is realistic? Alignment is not perfection. It is the willingness to notice, repair, and practice again.

SCENE 17: Use the Life Alignment Scan
A Life Alignment Scan helps a client see where values receive attention and where they have been crowded out. Review work, close relationships, family, health, home, finances, learning, rest, play, spirituality, and contribution as relevant. This is not a scorecard for a perfectly balanced life. Look for patterns: one role absorbing every resource, one neglected value creating resentment, or one area already providing evidence that change is possible.

SCENE 18: Personal truth is honest self-knowledge
Personal truth is not a claim that every feeling must be acted on or that other people must agree. It is honest self-knowledge: what I notice, what matters, what I can no longer pretend not to know, and what responsibility follows. A client may recognize that a role is unsustainable, a relationship needs a conversation, or a neglected interest matters. Coaching helps her test that truth against facts, values, consequences, commitments, and safety.

SCENE 20: Emotional courage is appropriately honest action
Courageous honesty is not telling everyone everything. It is sharing what is relevant, with appropriate people, at a thoughtful time, for a clear purpose. A client may need to state a preference, admit uncertainty, ask for help, or name a boundary. The coach explores risk and readiness without rewarding oversharing. Emotional courage includes discernment, consent, context, and the capacity to handle a response that may not be ideal.

SCENE 21: Differentiate preference, need, request, and boundary
Clients often call every desire a boundary or every need a demand. Clarify four categories. A preference describes what the client would like. A need names what supports wellbeing or integrity. A request asks another person for a specific behavior while leaving room for an answer. A boundary states what the client will do to protect a limit. Precise language reduces confusion and helps personal truth become relationally responsible.

SCENE 22: Truth needs translation
A breakthrough that never changes language or behavior may feel meaningful but remain incomplete. Translate insight into clean words, a proportionate action, and a review point. If a client values connection, she might protect one technology-free dinner. If she values creativity, she may schedule ninety minutes for unfinished work. If she acknowledges resentment, she may prepare a specific request. The action should fit the truth without demanding a total life reconstruction overnight.

SCENE 23: Case study · Meet Elena
Elena is forty-two, leads operations for a growing company, supports an aging parent, and is the person her family calls when something goes wrong. She is proud of her competence and loves her family. Yet she feels resentful, exhausted, and disconnected from creativity and friendship. She says, my life belongs to everyone else. She does not want to abandon responsibility. She wants to understand where devotion ended and automatic self-erasure began.

SCENE 24: Elena's identity constellation
Elena's map shows more than overload. As a leader she is organized and decisive. As a daughter she is devoted and vigilant. With friends she is humorous and curious. As an artist she is observant, patient, and almost entirely absent from her current schedule. The coach does not tell Elena to paint. The coach helps her notice that creativity represents aliveness and attention—qualities she misses across her whole life.

SCENE 25: Elena's values tension
Elena does not lack values; she has values in conflict. Care and reliability have dominated because they bring approval and prevent immediate problems. Creativity and freedom feel selfish because their benefits are less visible to others. The coaching task is not to replace care with freedom. It is to help Elena define care without self-erasure, reliability without overfunctioning, creativity without perfection, and freedom that includes responsible communication.

SCENE 27: From resentment to information
Resentment can signal an unspoken expectation, repeated overcommitment, unequal responsibility, or a choice the client has not acknowledged. It does not automatically prove exploitation, and it should not be dismissed as negativity. Ask what Elena keeps agreeing to, what she hopes others will notice without being told, what conversation she is avoiding, and what repair is hers. This returns agency without making her responsible for everyone else's behavior.

SCENE 28: Elena's first alignment experiment
Elena chooses a four-part experiment. She protects one two-hour block for unstructured creative work. She asks a sibling to own one recurring caregiving task. She tells her partner that constant availability is creating resentment and requests a weekly planning conversation. At week's end, she reviews guilt, relief, family responses, and what she learned. The coach helps her prepare for discomfort without treating discomfort as evidence the action was wrong.

SCENE 29: When others dislike the client's alignment
When a client changes a familiar pattern, other people may be confused, inconvenienced, hurt, or relieved. The coach should not label every negative response resistance, nor treat disapproval as proof that the client is wrong. Help the client listen for legitimate impact, honor commitments, distinguish discomfort from danger, and revise communication where needed. Integrity includes both self-respect and accountability for how choices affect others.

SCENE 30: Self-validation supports honest choice
Self-validation means acknowledging that a feeling and need are understandable. It does not certify every interpretation. Elena can say, it makes sense that I feel guilty because availability has been part of my identity. The guilt does not prove I am doing harm. I can check my commitments and choose responsibly. This stance reduces the demand for immediate reassurance and helps clients tolerate the uncertainty that often accompanies identity change.

SCENE 31: Coach identity change without creating dependency
Identity work can make a coach feel powerful. Avoid telling clients who they really are, announcing a hidden purpose, or becoming the person who grants permission to live. Reflect patterns, offer language tentatively, ask permission before challenge, and return decisions. A useful sentence is: I have an observation; would you like to hear it and decide whether it fits? The client remains the authority on lived experience and the owner of action.

SCENE 33: Scope boundary · identity coaching is not treatment
Coaching may explore roles, values, confidence, life transitions, and choices. It does not diagnose identity disorders, treat trauma, recover memories, manage suicidal thinking, or provide care for severe depression, panic, dissociation, addiction, or other significant impairment. If safety concerns or clinical needs appear, name the boundary calmly and support connection with qualified care. Referral is not rejection; it is an ethical match between need and service.

SCENE 34: A seven-question values conversation
When you need a sequence, ask: what matters most here? Which values are in tension? What role, rule, or expectation is shaping the choice? What does the current pattern protect, and what does it cost? What truth needs acknowledgment? What action would express the leading value responsibly? How and when will you review the result? Ask one question at a time. Follow the client's answer rather than racing through a checklist.

SCENE 35: Workbook · Build your Alignment Map
Choose a manageable, nonclinical area of your own life. Name the relevant roles, expectations, and values. Record what your current behavior demonstrates, not only what you intend. Identify the alignment gap without shaming yourself. Write one personal truth that includes facts and responsibility. Then design a small experiment with clean language, a realistic boundary or request, and a review date. This practice will help you coach the process with greater empathy.

SCENE 36: Practice assignment · Values under pressure
With a practice partner, explore one low-stakes decision involving competing values. The coach should reflect both sides without deciding which should win. Ask what each value protects, what each option costs, and what behavior would express the chosen priority. The observer tracks whether the coach imposed a value, confused a goal with a value, dismissed context, or moved too quickly. Finish with a client-owned experiment and a clear review question.

SCENE 37: The Roux Life standard
The Roux Life standard is clarity, courage, alignment, and responsibility. Values become visible in behavior. Identity remains larger than roles, achievements, or other people's approval. Personal truth is explored with context and translated into proportionate action. Emotional courage is honest without becoming reckless or performative. The coach protects client ownership and refers when needs extend beyond coaching.
