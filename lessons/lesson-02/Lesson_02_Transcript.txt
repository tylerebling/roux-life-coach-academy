ROUX LIFE ACADEMY
Lesson 2: The ROUX Coaching Method
Complete Narration Transcript

This transcript accompanies the interactive presentation. It is original Roux Life educational material. RLC is a private professional coaching certificate and is not a mental-health license.

SCENE 1: The ROUX Coaching Method
Welcome to the ROUX Coaching Method: Recognize, Open, Uncover, and Execute. This lesson gives beginning coaches a practical structure for guiding conversations without turning coaching into a rigid script. We will study each stage, watch the stages work together, practice choosing useful questions, and learn how to move toward action while preserving the client’s ownership.

SCENE 2: Your learning journey
We will begin by exploring why coaches need a framework. Then we will examine Recognize, Open, Uncover, and Execute one stage at a time. You will learn sample questions, observe a complete coaching conversation, and practice identifying what a client may need next. We will finish with common mistakes, ethical limits, and an application plan.

SCENE 3: Why beginning coaches need structure
Beginning coaches often believe they need a brilliant question every few seconds. That pressure makes genuine listening difficult. A framework reduces the need to perform. It gives the coach a reliable path while leaving room for the client’s story. Structure should make the coach more present, not more mechanical.

SCENE 4: A framework is not a script
A script can cause the coach to follow prepared words instead of the client. The ROUX Method works differently. It helps the coach notice what kind of work may be useful next. The stages can overlap, repeat, or occur in a different rhythm. The client’s readiness, consent, and needs remain more important than completing a sequence.

SCENE 5: The four-stage pathway
The method contains four stages. Recognize establishes the current reality. Open creates enough curiosity and emotional permission for honest exploration. Uncover identifies the values, beliefs, needs, fears, patterns, and practical conditions shaping the issue. Execute converts useful insight into a client-owned next step.

SCENE 6: The coach owns the process—not the answer
The coach is responsible for the integrity of the process. The client remains responsible for their truth, values, decisions, and actions. This division protects autonomy. A strong framework does not give the coach greater control over the client. It helps the coach offer a more disciplined form of support.

SCENE 7: R — Recognize
Recognize means understanding what is present before trying to improve it. Listen for the observable event, the client’s interpretation, emotional experience, repeated pattern, and immediate priority. The coach is not diagnosing the cause. The coach is helping the client describe the current reality clearly enough to choose a useful focus.

SCENE 8: Separate event, meaning, and emotion
Clients often blend event, meaning, and emotion into one conclusion. A manager did not invite the client to a meeting. The client believes this proves they are not respected. They feel embarrassed and angry. All three layers matter, but they are not identical. Separating them creates more options for exploration.

SCENE 9: Recognize the session goal
A broad life concern is rarely a workable session goal. Ask what would make today’s conversation useful. The client may want clarity, a decision, preparation for a conversation, a boundary, a small experiment, or accountability. A clear session goal prevents the conversation from becoming an endless review of everything that is wrong.

SCENE 10: Checkpoint: what belongs in Recognize?
Which response belongs in Recognize? The strongest answer is B: clarify the event, experience, and desired focus. Telling the client what the problem really is imposes the coach’s conclusion. Requiring immediate action moves too quickly. Recognition creates a shared understanding of where the conversation is beginning.

SCENE 11: O — Open
Open means creating conditions where the client can examine the issue honestly. This requires more than asking questions. Tone, pace, silence, nonjudgment, and permission all matter. The coach does not force vulnerability. The coach invites exploration and respects the client’s right to choose how deeply to engage.

SCENE 12: Questions that open thinking
Opening questions expand rather than corner. They help the client consider what has been ignored, assumed, or avoided. Use one question at a time. Allow silence. Do not stack five questions together. A thoughtful pause often gives the client more room than another clever prompt.

SCENE 13: Curiosity versus interrogation
Curiosity feels different from interrogation. Curious coaching is spacious and responsive. Interrogation is rapid, leading, and driven by the coach’s agenda. If the client begins giving short defensive answers, the coach should slow down, acknowledge the pace, and ask what would help the conversation feel more useful.

SCENE 14: Use reflections—not only questions
Coaching is not a continuous interview. Reflections help clients hear their own complexity. A coach may reflect a tension, a value, an emotion, or a repeated phrase. The reflection should remain tentative. Say, I wonder if, or, It sounds as though, rather than declaring that you know the client better than they know themselves.

SCENE 15: Checkpoint: which response opens?
The strongest opening response is B: what happens inside you when disagreement begins? It invites awareness without prescribing a solution. Option A judges and directs. Option C may eventually become a useful experiment, but it comes before the coach understands what conflict represents to the client.

SCENE 16: U — Uncover
Uncover is where the client identifies what is shaping the issue beneath the first description. This may include values, beliefs, needs, fears, protective patterns, consequences, resources, or real-world constraints. Uncovering is not searching for hidden trauma. It is identifying what is relevant to the client’s present choice.

SCENE 17: The visible problem and the deeper drivers
A client may ask for better time management, yet another planner may not solve the actual problem. The pattern may be driven by perfectionism, unclear priorities, exhaustion, fear of judgment, or commitments the client does not want. The coach remains open to practical and emotional explanations rather than assuming one hidden cause.

SCENE 18: Questions that uncover meaning
Uncovering questions explore meaning while staying within coaching scope. Ask what the client may be protecting, which value feels threatened, what cost is already being paid, or what need has not been acknowledged. Avoid labeling the client with a disorder, attachment style, or trauma response without appropriate clinical qualifications.

SCENE 19: Listen for competing commitments
Clients are not always blocked by a lack of motivation. They may be committed to two important things that conflict. A client wants success and fears visibility. Another wants connection and protects independence. Naming both commitments reduces shame and helps the client design a response that respects more of what matters.

SCENE 20: The belief-testing sequence
Belief testing does not require replacing every difficult thought with a positive statement. Begin by naming the belief. Examine supporting and complicating evidence. Identify what the belief causes the client to do. Then develop a more useful belief to test through behavior. The replacement should be credible, not artificially cheerful.

SCENE 21: Scenario: Elena wants to start a business
Elena wants to start a consulting business. She makes progress and stops whenever the work becomes visible. She describes herself as undisciplined. A productivity plan may help later, but first the coach should explore what progress means. Success could bring judgment, family conflict, new responsibility, or an identity change that feels risky.

SCENE 22: Choose the strongest uncovering question
The strongest response is B. It explores desire and resistance together. The other questions belong closer to execution. If the coach moves into scheduling too soon, Elena may create another plan without understanding why previous plans became difficult at the moment progress felt real.

SCENE 23: X — Execute
Execute turns useful awareness into behavior. The next step may be an action, conversation, boundary, decision, experiment, information-gathering task, or referral. Execution should not be rushed. An impressive plan the client does not own is less valuable than a modest step the client is genuinely prepared to take.

SCENE 24: An action step that can survive real life
Use five tests for an action step. Is it specific enough to understand? Realistic within the client’s current life? Meaningful rather than busywork? Chosen by the client? Connected to what the conversation revealed? A vague promise such as communicate better becomes more useful when converted into a particular conversation, time, request, and preparation plan.

SCENE 25: Prepare for interference
A plan is incomplete until the client considers interference. Ask what could get in the way, what early signal they will notice, and how they want to respond. Identify support and a smaller viable version. This turns accountability into preparation instead of punishment after the fact.

SCENE 26: Accountability without shame
When an action is missed, remain curious. The plan may have been too large, the obstacle underestimated, the goal unimportant, or fear activated. Accountability reviews what happened, extracts learning, and adjusts. It does not make the coach a disappointed parent or the client a failing student.

SCENE 27: ROUX is a loop—not a straight line
Real coaching rarely moves in a perfect line. During action planning, a new fear may appear. Return to Recognize or Uncover. If the client feels rushed, reopen the conversation. The framework supports intentional movement, but presence determines when to advance, pause, or return.

SCENE 28: A complete ROUX conversation
Here is the framework in motion. Recognize reflects the client’s current experience. Open invites honest exploration. Uncover identifies the fear beneath the pattern. Execute converts insight into a specific request. The coach does not decide who should take responsibility or force the conversation. The client develops and owns the action.

SCENE 29: Common mistakes to avoid
Common mistakes include skipping recognition, forcing vulnerability, searching endlessly for a hidden root cause, and rushing toward action. The method is not a diagnostic tool. It cannot tell the coach whether a client has trauma, a disorder, or a clinical condition. The coach should not become more invested in change than the client.

SCENE 30: Scope and referral remain active in every stage
Scope does not become a separate concern at the end of a session. It remains active in every stage. If a client describes immediate danger, self-harm, severe impairment, abuse, acute addiction, or symptoms requiring assessment and treatment, ordinary coaching should pause. Use appropriate referral, emergency, and safeguarding procedures.

SCENE 31: Build your first ROUX map
Use your workbook to build a complete ROUX map for a manageable coaching issue. Write the presenting concern and two questions for each of the first three stages. Then design one possible execution step while remembering that the real client must choose it. Finish by identifying any scope concern that would require caution or referral.

SCENE 32: Reflection: where will you be tempted to rush?
Every coach has tendencies. You may rush toward advice, emotional depth, a perfect plan, or away from silence and difficult feelings. Awareness of your tendency protects the client. Choose one behavior you will practice, such as allowing five seconds of silence or asking permission before moving deeper.

SCENE 33: The Roux Life standard
The ROUX Coaching Method offers structure without control, depth without diagnosis, challenge without shame, action without rushing, and accountability without punishment. Complete the checkpoints, coaching scenario, workbook map, and twelve-question quiz. A passing score is eighty percent. In the next lesson, we will explore stories, beliefs, and protective patterns.
